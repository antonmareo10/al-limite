Spanish public contracts · 2023–2025

The €14,999 wallLegal limit: €15,000

Below €15,000, a Spanish public body can hire a company directly, without a tender. We looked at 1.47 million of those small contracts to see what that line does to prices.

By Antonio González Fernández · Data: Spain's Public Sector Contracting Platform · Figures generated 15 November 2026

€14,999

Not fifteen thousand euros. One euro less.

Between 2023 and 2025, Spanish public bodies signed 2,885 contracts for exactly that amount. It isn't even the most popular price. These are the eight most common prices in the last €1,000 before the limit:

  • €14,9004,861 contracts
  • €14,5003,334 contracts
  • €14,9503,023 contracts
  • €14,9992,885 contracts
  • €14,9902,728 contracts
  • €14,8002,509 contracts
  • €14,8501,596 contracts
  • €14,9951,365 contracts

Any one of these prices is perfectly legal. Put together, they make a shape. This story is about that shape.

One signature

Spanish law calls them contratos menores, minor contracts. If a service or a supply costs less than €15,000 before VAT, or building works less than €40,000, a public body can choose a supplier and sign (Law 9/2017, article 118). Above the line, it normally has to advertise the job and let other companies compete for it.

Under €15,000
  1. Pick a supplier
  2. Sign
€15,000 or more
  1. Advertise the job
  2. Receive offers
  3. Compare and award

Splitting one job into smaller contracts to stay under the line is against the law (article 99.2). But you can't see a split by looking at one contract: each one looks fine on its own. If there's a pattern, it only shows up in the crowd.

So we gathered the crowd: the minor contracts for services, supplies and works published on Spain's national contracting platform and awarded between 2023 and 2025. That's 1,374,964 for services and supplies and 95,831 for works, from 7,663 contracting bodies: town councils, ministries, universities, public companies.

Guess the last bar

Here are the services and supplies contracts from €6,000 up, counted in €200 price bands. As prices rise, the bars get shorter: expensive purchases are rarer than cheap ones. The little spikes sit on round prices, like €10,000 and €12,000. People like round numbers.

One bar is missing: the last one before the limit, from €14,800 to €15,000. How tall do you think it is?

38,435 contracts. That's 10.1 times the average €200 band between €9,000 and €14,000. And right after the line, from €15,000 to €15,200, there are just 204.

That's the wall. Prices don't fade out as they approach the limit. They pile up against it.

How many is too many?

A pile-up is easy to see. Measuring it takes a baseline: how many contracts would sit in the last €1,000 if the limit had no pull at all?

Take the five €1,000 bands before the last one, from €9,000 to €14,000. Together they hold 95,043 contracts.

If the limit didn't matter, the last band would hold about as many as an average band before it: 19,009. That's our expected number.

It's a generous baseline. Expensive purchases are rarer than cheap ones, so the true expectation for the last band is probably lower.

The last band holds 68,251.

That's 3.59 times what we'd expect: 49,242 more contracts than the baseline, in three years.

Chance alone can't produce a gap like this with so many contracts. The 95% confidence interval for the ratio runs from 3.56 to 3.63.

Maybe people just like round numbers?

Prices do cluster on round numbers. Could the wall be one more round-number spike, just a bigger one?

One way to check is to pretend the limit is somewhere else. We moved a fake limit every €500 from €7,000 to €18,000 and ran the same calculation each time: the €1,000 under the fake line against the five €1,000 bands before it.

At every fake line from €7,000 to €13,500, the ratio stays below 1, between 0.33 and 0.94: fewer contracts than the baseline, just as you'd expect when expensive purchases are rarer. Even at €10,000, the roundest number in the range, it's 0.61.

The ratio only shoots up at the real limit: 3.59. The fake lines €500 either side share part of its window, so they rise too. From €16,000 on, the ratio drops to 0.04 or less, because above the line a contract can no longer be a minor one.

Every year, every kind of body

If the wall came from one chaotic year, or from one corner of the public sector, it would show up in some slices and not in others. It shows up in all of them.

For services and supplies, the ratio was 3.58, 3.58 and 3.61 in the three years. Local government, regional governments, central government and other public bodies such as universities all sit between 3.26 and 4.23. For works, no type of body is below 3.53.

Month by month, the services ratio never drops below 3.37 or climbs above 3.75. December, when leftover budgets get spent, gets 9.5% of all services and supplies contracts and 10.6% of those just under the limit. The wall is there all year round.

The same wall, at €40,000

Building works have a higher limit: €40,000. The pattern doesn't care.

In the last €2,500 before the limit there are 13,484 works contracts, where 3,171 would be expected: 4.25 times as many (95% interval 4.15–4.35). The final €500 band alone, from €39,500 to €40,000, holds 6,899.

The single most common price in that stretch is €39,669.42, with 267 contracts. It looks random until you add Spain's 21% VAT. Then it comes to exactly €48,000: a round budget that, before tax, fits just under the line.

Three bodies, three shapes

A national total hides very different places. The search tool gives every contracting body its own chart. Here are three of them. We describe them by type and region rather than by name, because a statistical signal is no reason to single anyone out; every contract links to its official record.

  • A city council in Galicia. 163 contracts in the last €1,000, where 21.4 were expected. Ratio 7.62 (95% interval 5.93–9.81). Label: Bunching signal.
  • A university in the Basque Country. Ratio 2.15 (1.80–2.58). Label: Weak hint. Universities partly play by other rules: some research contracts can be minor up to a higher limit, so a wall at €15,000 is less expected there.
  • A university in the Valencian Community. 15,410 contracts, ratio 1.35 (0.89–2.00). Label: No signal. There's a bump in the very last €200 band, but across the last €1,000 the count is within what chance allows: the interval includes 1. The wall is common, but it isn't inevitable.

Notice how wide the intervals are compared with the national one. With fewer contracts, chance plays a bigger part. That's why the tool never ranks a body by its ratio alone, but by the cautious end of the interval.

A closer look

Zoom into the city council. Each dot below is one of the 150 most recent of its contracts in the last €1,000, placed by award date and price.

97 different companies won contracts in this band. 15 of them won three or more, 66 contracts between them. The two at the top, which we call A and B, won 7 each, spread across every year from 2023 to 2025.

That proves nothing by itself. Each contract may be a different job, needed at a different time. But it is the kind of pattern that deserves a question, and every one of these contracts has a public record anyone can open.

How common is it?

Of the 6,923 contracting bodies with services and supplies contracts, 5,680 have too few contracts in the two bands to judge. Of the 1,243 that remain, 684 (55%) show a clear signal: even on the most cautious reading of the interval, at least twice as many contracts as expected sit in the last €1,000.

It isn't one kind of purchase either. Sorted by what they buy, all 44 product families with enough contracts sit above 1, even at the low end of their intervals. IT services lead, at 5.49.

What this does not prove

A wall is a signal, not a verdict. It tells you where to look, not what you'll find.

  • It doesn't identify split contracts. That takes reading the files: whether several contracts meet the same need, and whether they could have been planned together. Open data doesn't say.
  • A price near the limit can be perfectly reasonable. A budget capped in advance, market prices, or a job sized to what a small office can handle quickly all push prices towards the line, legally.
  • The legal limit applies to the estimated value. We use the award amount, the figure published most reliably.
  • Not everyone has the same limit. Public research bodies, such as universities, can treat some contracts as minor up to a higher amount (Law 9/2017, 54th additional provision).
  • The data is messy. Each body records its own contracts. There are VAT-inclusive amounts in the VAT-exclusive field, impossible dates, and 9,856 “minor” services and supplies contracts above €15,000. Only 48.5% say what they buy.
  • It doesn't cover all of Spain. Bodies that publish on their own regional platforms (in Catalonia, the Basque Country, Madrid, Andalusia or Galicia, for example) are not in this dataset.

So, is the wall a problem?

There are two ways to read it, and the data can't choose between them.

It's efficiency

The limit exists so that small purchases are quick. A town hall with a small staff can't run a tender for every job, so it uses all the room the law gives it. A price just under the line may simply be the most that can be done the quick way.

It's a leak

Every contract sized to fit under the line is one where no other company got the chance to offer a better price. Over three years that's 49,242 more contracts in the last €1,000 than the baseline predicts.

Both can be true, in different places. A national chart can't tell them apart. People who know a town can.

How this was made

The data. Spain's Public Sector Contracting Platform publishes minor contracts as open data, in yearly files of XML feeds. We downloaded the four files for 2023–2026 (1.08 GB, 4,196 feed files), read 2,062,794 entries, kept the latest version of each of 1,994,466 distinct contracts, dropped cancelled ones, and analysed the 1,470,795 services, supplies and works contracts awarded from 2023 to 2025. The 2026 file is used only to pick up late corrections.

The statistics. Python, NumPy and SciPy. The near band is compared with a reference band five times as wide just below it. Counts are treated as Poisson, which gives an exact (Clopper-Pearson) 95% interval for each ratio. Thousands of bodies are tested at once, so p-values are corrected for multiple comparisons (Benjamini-Hochberg). A body is labelled “bunching signal” only with at least 30 contracts in the two bands, 10 near the limit, a q-value below 0.01, and a lower bound of 2 or more. A sweep of fake limits and checks by year and month guard against artefacts.

The AI, and how it is kept honest. The search tool explains every flagged body in plain Spanish and English. Those explanations are written by a small AI model, Qwen3-4B, running locally on a laptop with llama.cpp: no paid service, nothing sent to anyone. The model never computes or rewrites a number. Code writes every figure as an exact sentence; the model chooses which facts matter and writes the caveats in its own words; then a verifier checks every sentence against the computed facts and rejects anything that doesn't match or that uses accusatory language. The model never sees a company's name, so it can't repeat one. Of the 1,786 texts, 1,620 came from the model and passed; 166 fell back to a fixed template, labelled as such. The verifier rejected 1,208 drafts on the way.

This page. Built by a student, with the help of an AI coding assistant (Claude). Every number on it is read from the project's result files by a script, and a test fails if any of them drifts. The charts are hand-written SVG, with no libraries; every chart has a data table behind it.
