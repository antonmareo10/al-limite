One signature

Spanish law calls them contratos menores, minor contracts. If a service or a supply costs less than {{lim_ss}} before VAT, or building works less than {{lim_ob}}, a public body can choose a supplier and sign (Law 9/2017, article 118). Above the line, it normally has to advertise the job and let other companies compete for it.

Under {{lim_ss}}
  1. Pick a supplier
  2. Sign
{{lim_ss}} or more
  1. Advertise the job
  2. Receive offers
  3. Compare and award

Splitting one job into smaller contracts to stay under the line is against the law (article 99.2). But you can't see a split by looking at one contract: each one looks fine on its own. If there's a pattern, it only shows up in the crowd.

So I gathered the crowd: the minor contracts for services, supplies and works published on Spain's national contracting platform and awarded between {{y0}} and {{y1}}. That's {{n_ss}} for services and supplies and {{n_obras}} for works, from {{n_org}} contracting bodies: town councils, ministries, universities, public companies.

How many is too many?

A pile-up is easy to see. Measuring it takes a baseline: how many contracts would sit in the last {{near_w_ss}} if the limit had no pull at all?

Maybe people just like round numbers?

Prices do cluster on round numbers. Could the wall be one more round-number spike, just a bigger one?

One way to check is to pretend the limit is somewhere else. I moved a fake limit every {{fake_step}} from {{fake_from}} to {{fake_to}} and ran the same calculation each time: the {{near_w_ss}} under the fake line against the {{k_ss}} {{near_w_ss}} bands before it.

At every fake line from {{fake_from}} to {{fake_low_to}}, the ratio stays below 1, between {{fake_low_min}} and {{fake_low_max}}: fewer contracts than the baseline, just as you'd expect when expensive purchases are rarer. Even at {{round_line}}, the roundest number in the range, it's {{r_round}}.

The ratio only shoots up at the real limit: {{razon}}. The fake lines {{fake_overlap}} either side share part of its window, so they rise too. From {{fake_high_from}} on, the ratio drops to {{fake_high_max}} or less, because above the line a contract can no longer be a minor one.

Every year, every kind of body

If the wall came from one chaotic year, or from one corner of the public sector, it would show up in some slices and not in others. It shows up in all of them.

What this does not prove

A wall is a signal, not a verdict. It tells you where to look, not what you'll find.

  • It doesn't identify split contracts. That takes reading the files: whether several contracts meet the same need, and whether they could have been planned together. Open data doesn't say.
  • A price near the limit can be perfectly reasonable. A budget capped in advance, market prices, or a job sized to what a small office can handle quickly all push prices towards the line, legally.
  • The legal limit applies to the estimated value. I use the award amount, the figure published most reliably.
  • Not everyone has the same limit. Public research bodies, such as universities, can treat some contracts as minor up to a higher amount (Law 9/2017, 54th additional provision).
  • The data is messy. Each body records its own contracts. There are VAT-inclusive amounts in the VAT-exclusive field, impossible dates, and {{ss_sobre}} “minor” services and supplies contracts above {{lim_ss}}. Only {{cpv_pct}}% say what they buy.
  • It doesn't cover all of Spain. Bodies that publish on their own regional platforms (in Catalonia, the Basque Country, Madrid, Andalusia or Galicia, for example) are not in this dataset.

So, is the wall a problem?

There are two ways to read it, and the data can't choose between them.

It's efficiency

The limit exists so that small purchases are quick. A town hall with a small staff can't run a tender for every job, so it uses all the room the law gives it. A price just under the line may simply be the most that can be done the quick way.

It's a leak

Every contract sized to fit under the line is one where no other company got the chance to offer a better price. Over {{n_years}} years that's {{excess}} more contracts in the last {{near_w_ss}} than the baseline predicts.

Both can be true, in different places. A national chart can't tell them apart. People who know a town can.

How I made this

Three things went wrong or were hard along the way.

  • The AI got the numbers right and the meaning wrong. The search tool explains each flagged body in plain Spanish and English. I used {{ia_modelo}} for that, a small open model that runs on my laptop with {{ia_runtime}}, so nothing's sent to anyone. In my first version I let the model write the paragraph about the data, and a check made sure every number in it was one the code had computed. Drafts passed that check and were still wrong. A ratio turned into a share of contracts. A price band was the wrong one. So I took the numbers away from it: code now writes every figure as a fixed sentence, and the model only picks which ones to show, then says in its own words what the data can't prove and what to check. It never sees a company's name.
  • Even boxed in, it failed {{ia_rech}} times. A script checks every draft before it's published, and it threw out {{rech_en}} in English and {{rech_es}} in Spanish. Most of them, {{rech_cifras}}, had slipped a number into a part meant to have none. Some made things up: {{rech_cita}} cited a contract that doesn't exist. After {{max_intentos}} failed drafts a body gets a fixed template instead, and the page says so. That happened to {{ia_plantilla}} of the {{ia_textos}} texts, {{tpl_en}} of them in English; the other {{ia_modelo_n}} came from the model and passed. Only {{first_try}} of the {{ia_fichas}} flagged bodies got through first time in both languages. I've kept every rejected draft, with the reason it failed, in explicaciones.json.
  • The data is a feed, not a table. The platform publishes a contract again every time it changes, and cancellations come as separate notices. I downloaded the {{n_files}} yearly files for {{file_from}}–{{file_to}} ({{gb}} GB, {{atom}} feed files) and read {{leidas}} entries. Keep only the latest version of each and you get {{distintos}} distinct contracts. Then I dropped the cancelled ones. The {{file_to}} file's only there to catch late corrections. This story counts the {{n_total}} services, supplies and works contracts among them that were awarded from {{y0}} to {{y1}}. Some records are still plainly wrong. The caveats above say how.

The statistics. Python, NumPy and SciPy. The near band is compared with a reference band {{k_ss}} times as wide just below it. Counts are treated as Poisson, which gives an exact (Clopper-Pearson) 95% interval for each ratio. Thousands of bodies are tested at once, so p-values are corrected for multiple comparisons (Benjamini-Hochberg). A body is labelled “bunching signal” only with at least {{min_ventana}} contracts in the two bands, {{min_cerca}} near the limit, a q-value below {{q_max}}, and a lower bound of {{ic_inf_min}} or more. A sweep of fake limits and checks by year and month guard against artefacts.

Who did what. This is a one-person student project. I directed it, and Claude Code, an AI coding tool, wrote much of the code behind the search tool and this page. It drafted much of this text too. Every number on the page is read from the project's result files by a script, and a test fails if any of them drifts. The charts are hand-written SVG with no libraries. Each one has a data table behind it.
